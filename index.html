I Couldn't Control What My Kids Saw Online — This 160-Year-Old Trick Changed Everything

As a worried parent, I wanted something that could pull my kids away from screens without another fight.

Like most parents, I was already worried about how much time my kids were spending on screens.

But what bothered me even more was something much harder to control: what happened once they were online.

It wasn't just videos and games. They were being exposed to opinions, beliefs, trends and ideas I never chose for them — often without me there to explain or put any of it into context.

And that was what really bothered me. The problem wasn't just how much they were watching. It was what they were watching.

Because some of the content reaching kids today isn't harmless entertainment. Research has linked exposure to certain types of online content with changes in behavior, anxiety, depression, body image and other mental-health concerns.

Research from Common Sense Media, the American Psychological Association, and the U.S. Surgeon General has raised concerns about the content children are exposed to online and its potential impact on mental health.

I did what most parents do — set limits, turn on parental controls and check their devices whenever I could. But realistically, I still couldn't control every video, every message, every recommendation or every person they came across.

As a pilot, I'm used to thinking about risk and control. But this was different.

Then I realized something: I might not be able to control everything my kids came across online — but I could give them a reason to spend less time there.

Not by taking the screen away or starting another fight. I needed to find something they would actually want to choose instead.

And if I could do that, maybe I could get back some of the time screens were taking away from us.

At that point, I knew I needed to solve three things: get my kids off screens, give them a reason to stay off, and find a way to reconnect with them.

Getting Them Off Screens Was The Easy Part

I started with the obvious alternatives — new toys, board games, weekend activities and little projects around the house.

Most of them worked for a while. A new toy might hold their attention for a few days. A board game would come out a handful of times. A weekend activity was fun while we were doing it. Then the interest faded. Before long, everything started collecting dust.

That was the pattern I kept running into. It wasn't enough to find something that could pull my kids away from a screen once. I needed something they would actually want to come back to — and something we could do together.

The One Thing That Still Had Their Attention

For all the things competing for my kids' attention, there was still one subject that always seemed to pull them in: my job as a pilot.

They wanted to know about the airplanes, how they flew, where I was going and what it was like inside the cockpit. Whenever I talked about flying, they actually paid attention.

That got me thinking. Instead of buying another toy that might end up collecting dust, maybe I could bring a little piece of aviation home.

So I grabbed some paper and started folding airplanes with them. Nothing complicated, nothing electronic — just paper, a few folds and a simple challenge: "Whose plane can fly the farthest?"

They Didn't Ask For Their Screens Back

I expected we'd fold a few planes, throw them around for a while and move on.

Instead, one flight turned into a challenge. They wanted to see if they could get more distance, make a plane fly straighter or beat the last throw. Before long, we were adjusting the wings, changing little things and comparing what happened each time.

No screens. No batteries. Just a table full of planes and a reason to keep trying.

I had found something they actually wanted to choose instead.

I Finally Found Something Better Than Screen Time: Flight Time

It wasn't really about the paper airplanes themselves. It was about what happened around them. We were talking more, laughing more, competing, learning and spending time together without me having to force any of it.

Every flight gave us something new to work on, whether it was getting more distance, fixing a bad throw or trying to beat the last one. My son started calling it Flight Time.

I Set Out To Reinvent The 160-Year-Old Paper Airplane

Paper airplanes have been around for more than 100 years, but the basic experience has barely changed: fold one, throw it, and eventually move on.

I didn't want five paper airplanes that just looked different. I wanted five aircraft that actually flew differently. So instead of starting with the artwork, I started with the flight.

Each aircraft had to have one specific job — speed, glide, distance, stunts or precision. Small differences in the folds, wing shape, symmetry, balance — even the paper itself — could completely change how an aircraft flew.

I also wanted the planes to feel connected, so each aircraft got its own identity, mission and collectible card. That was the beginning of what would eventually become Hangar 28.

The Hangar 28 fleet

Five Aircraft. Five Missions. Two Of Each.

One day we could race for distance, another we could see who could stay in the air the longest, and the next we could set up a target and see who got closest. I also included two sheets of every aircraft.

At first, the second sheet was practical. If something went wrong, we could rebuild it and try again.

But it quickly became one of my favorite parts of the idea.

My kids could build one, I could build the other, and we could compete with the exact same plane.

Same design. Same challenge. No excuses.

And because each aircraft had its own mission and collectible card, the five planes started to feel less like separate paper airplanes and more like a fleet we could build together.

Would They Still Choose Flight Time Over Screen Time?

I didn't take the screens away or force it. Over the next three weekends, I just watched to see what would happen once the newness wore off.

Weekend 1

That first weekend, I didn’t take the screens away or try to turn Hangar 28 into some big family activity. I just left the planes out and watched to see if the kids would come back to them on their own.

They did. And what stuck with me was the change in what I started hearing. Instead of “one more episode” or “one more video,” it became “one more throw.”

That was the first sign this might actually hold their attention longer than the toys and activities we'd tried before.

Weekend 2

By the second weekend, the kids were back into school and I’d had a long week of flying. I was tired, and the weather wasn’t great, so going outside wasn’t really an option.

Normally, that would have been an easy screen day.

Instead, we turned the living room into our own little airport.

The kids put on my pilot hat, picked their aircraft and started pretending they were running real flights. A rainy afternoon turned the living room into our own little airport.

The kids put on my pilot hat, picked their aircraft and started pretending they were running real flights.

I even taught them some of the language pilots use when talking to the tower, so one of us would act like air traffic control while the other called in a flight, asked for clearance and tried to land on the target.

Before long, they weren’t just throwing paper airplanes anymore. They were playing pilot, learning how aviation worked and building an entire game around it.

That was when I started seeing how much bigger Flight Time could be than the planes themselves.

Weekend 3

By the third weekend, I was away on a long-haul flight, so my wife was home with the kids.

She got one of the planes built, but it kept diving almost straight into the ground.

She scanned the QR code and opened the Hangar 28 AI-powered flight assistant. She simply told it what the plane was doing, and the AI walked her through a few adjustments and pulled up the exact tuning video for that aircraft.

After a couple of small changes, the plane was flying properly again, and they were back outside testing it together. My wife sent me this while I was away on a long-haul flight.

When I got home, they couldn’t wait to tell me how Mom did.

That was probably the clearest sign yet that Flight Time had become bigger than something Dad did with the kids.

It had become something our family could keep coming back to — even when I wasn’t home.

Is Flight Time Really Better Than Screen Time?

I wasn't trying to eliminate screens from their lives. I just finally felt like we had something that could compete with them.

Flight Time gave us a reason to build, learn, compete, use our imagination and actually spend that time together — even on the weekends I wasn't home.
