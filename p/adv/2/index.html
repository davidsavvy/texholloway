The More Money I Spent, The Less Fun It Felt Like We Were Having.

"Movies, restaurants, arcades, games — every weekend meant another plan and another expense."

After a while, I started wondering why I was putting so much into our weekends and still not enjoying them that much.

I work construction, and after five days of early mornings and long hours on my feet, I'm usually pretty beat by Friday.

All I really want is to crack open a cold beer, sit down for a little while and let my brain shut off.

Instead, before the weekend even starts, I'm already thinking about where we're going, what we're doing and how much it's going to cost.

Taking the four of us to a movie could easily cost over $100 once you added tickets, popcorn and drinks.

A simple dinner out could run $150 for the four of us without doing anything fancy.

And if the kids still had energy to burn afterward, one more stop at an arcade or activity center could turn a pretty normal Saturday into a $200+ day.

I just kept thinking about how fast those Saturdays added up, and how that same money could have gone toward savings, a family trip, or the kids' college funds.

After a while, I started wondering if all that spending was even making our weekends better.

One Saturday, I remember standing in the middle of an arcade holding two half-finished drinks while the kids bounced from one game to the next.

I'd already paid for the game cards, lunch and parking, and at some point I caught myself checking the time to see how much longer we were staying.

The kids were having fun, but it came in little ten-minute bursts — one game, another swipe, another round, then onto the next thing.

And I wasn't really part of it either.

I was usually the one driving, paying, carrying the drinks and making sure everyone was where they were supposed to be.

At some point, I realized I was managing the day more than enjoying it.

I was spending more and more trying to create good family time, but somehow I felt less and less like I was actually part of it.

A few nights later, I found myself thinking about my own dad and how different weekends felt when I was a kid.

He wasn't standing off to the side managing everything while I had all the fun. Most of the time, he was right there doing it with me.

We threw paper airplanes across the living room, played catch at the park, raced bikes down the street, and somehow turned almost everything into a competition.

None of it was some big activity my dad had planned for me.

Looking back, those are still some of the weekends I remember most.

That's when it hit me: My dad never had to spend over $200 to make a Saturday feel like a Saturday.

The next evening, I pulled a few sheets of printer paper out of the desk and called the kids over to the kitchen table.

“This was one of the things Grandpa and I used to do,” I told them as I started folding the kind of paper airplane I remembered from when I was their age.

For a second, it felt strange how familiar it all was — same paper, same folds, just a different generation sitting at the table.

What started with a few sheets of printer paper turned into an hour of folding, testing, competing and actually enjoying the time together.

A few minutes later, all three of us had a plane ready to go and we were launching them down the hallway.

One kept nose-diving into the floor, another flew surprisingly far, and pretty soon everybody had an opinion about whose was best.

I started tweaking the wings and showing them little tricks I remembered from when I was a kid.

“I remember Grandpa showing me this,” I said, bending the back of one wing up a little to see what it would do.

Before long, the three of us were testing things, making changes and trying to beat each other — and I realized I was having just as much fun as they were.

Almost an hour had gone by and I hadn't checked the time once.

I wasn't driving, paying or managing anything — I was just there with them, having fun.

No tickets, no reservations, no $200 Saturday — just a few sheets of printer paper and three people trying to prove they could build the better plane.

My wife saw how much fun we were having and started looking on Pinterest for other simple things we could do together.

That's when she came across something called Hangar 28.

She sent it to me and said,

“Look at this. It's like what you guys were doing, but way cooler.”

Not just homemade airplanes—five aircraft built to fly differently and compete side by side.

At first, I figured it was just a nicer version of the paper airplanes we'd already been making.

But when I looked closer, there were five different planes — one for speed, distance, gliding, tricks and accuracy.

What really caught my attention was that there were two of each one, so the kids and I could build the same plane and compete with each other.

A lot of toys are really just for the kids, not something you actually do with them.

With this, the whole point was to build the same plane, throw them side by side and see who won.

The more I looked at them, the more I realized these weren't the regular paper airplanes I grew up folding.

They all had completely different shapes depending on what they were meant to do — speed, glide, distance, tricks or accuracy.

So instead of throwing the same plane over and over, there was something new to figure out with each one.

Every plane gave us something different to compete over, so it didn't feel like we'd build them once and be done.

I could see us racing one night, trying for distance the next, then arguing over who could actually hit a target.

So we decided to give it a shot and ordered one.

Honestly, I wasn't expecting some life-changing toy.

I just figured if it gave us a few more nights like the one we'd had with those printer-paper planes, it was worth trying.

The First Weekend

When it arrived, we ended up right back at the same kitchen table where we'd made those first printer-paper planes.

Only this time there were five completely different aircraft sitting in front of us, and the kids were already debating which one looked the coolest.

“Two of every aircraft means nobody has to sit out.”

We decided to start with the speed plane and settle the important question first: who could make theirs fly faster?

The video walked us through it step by step, so I could build right alongside the kids instead of running the whole thing.

Once they were finished, we headed straight for the hallway and lined them up.

The first throw immediately turned into, “That one didn't count,” followed by a rematch.

Pretty soon we were tweaking the wings, throwing again and trying to figure out what made one plane fly better than the other.

By the time we finally stopped, the kids were already asking which plane we were doing next.

Best of all, I didn't have to plan whatever came next — they were asking me to do it with them.

The Next Saturday Was Different

Saturday came around, and normally that's when I start hearing, “What are we doing today?” or “Are we going anywhere?”

This time, one of the kids grabbed the Hangar and said, “Dad, let's do another one.”

For once, nobody cared about going anywhere — they just wanted me to sit down and play with them again.

We laid the rest of the planes out on the table and started picking through them.

This time we had a few choices — see who could fly the farthest, stay in the air the longest, do a trick or hit a target.

We finally settled on the distance plane and headed outside to see who could throw theirs the farthest.

Of course, one round wasn't enough, so we kept tweaking them and trying again.

Then my son wanted to try the accuracy plane, so pretty soon we were taking turns trying to hit a target.

At some point I realized we'd been at it for hours and nobody had asked what we were doing next.

We were just moving from one challenge to another, talking trash and having fun with it.

Staying home didn't feel like the backup plan anymore.

Then We Took It to a Birthday Party

A little while later, we were heading to a birthday party and the kids insisted on bringing the Hangar.

I honestly thought they might forget about it once they got there.

Instead, the second a couple of planes came out, the other kids wanted in too.

Pretty soon, they were taking turns throwing them, comparing whose went farther and trying to beat each other.

What made me laugh was seeing the same competitions we'd been having at home suddenly spread to all the other kids.

“Watching them all get into it, I remember thinking, this would actually make a pretty good birthday gift.”

Up until then, part of me still wondered if my kids just happened to like it.

But watching a bunch of other kids get pulled into the same races and challenges kind of answered that for me.

Something about building something simple and then competing over it just worked.

That was probably the biggest thing I took away from all of it.

I didn't need less family time. I just needed family time that didn't feel like another job.

I'd spent so long trying to create good weekends that I forgot the whole point was to actually enjoy them with my family.

Turns out, a good weekend didn't need tickets, reservations or some big plan.

The kids just needed something they actually wanted to do, and I needed something I could be part of too.

I'd spent plenty of money on things the kids enjoyed for an hour and then completely forgot about.

This was different because it gave us something we could keep coming back to, and I actually wanted to be part of it.

Compared to what I was already spending on a normal Saturday, that started to make a lot more sense to me.

A movie for the four of us could disappear in two hours and cost over $100.

Dinner and one more stop afterward could easily turn into a $200 day.

“What mattered wasn't spending less. It was finally part of the fun.”

Compared to that, spending $49.99 on something we could keep pulling back out and doing together felt pretty reasonable.

And yeah, I had the same thought you probably do: couldn't we just make paper airplanes ourselves?

Technically, sure — that's how this whole thing started in the first place.

But what we made at home was one plane and one little competition. v Hangar 28 had five different planes, and each one was built for a different kind of flying — speed, glide, distance, tricks or accuracy.

So instead of making one plane and throwing it over and over, we had something different to figure out and compete over each time.

The value wasn't really in the sheets of paper — it was in having something the kids actually wanted me to sit down and do with them.

That was the thing I'd been trying to find all along.

“If it doesn't bring your family back to the table, send it back. No questions asked.”

Looking back, I think my dad had it right.

The weekends I remember most weren't the expensive ones — they were the ones where we were actually doing something together.

Now, when my kids ask what we're doing this weekend, I don't feel like I need some big plan to have a good answer.
